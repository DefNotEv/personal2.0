---
title: I can't believe I'm defending NBE3U
date: 2025-10
version: all-around
---

If you were born in 2007 and attended high school in Ontario, you probably remember finding out that Grade 11 English was being replaced with "Understanding Contemporary First Nations, Métis, and Inuit Voices"

I was **PISSED.**

Don't get me wrong. Reconciliation is an incredibly important part of repairing Canada's relationship with Indigenous peoples, and I understood why we needed to learn about it. But come on, an *entire* English course? Not to mention, we were going to be the guinea pigs for a completely new curriculum.

I received the lowest English grade of my entire academic career in that class. Part of that was because the book I was assigned for my final project was, in my opinion, terribly written. I was the only person in the entire class to critcize their final book.

My teacher told me I hadn't provided enough evidence to support my argument.

I disagree. But I digress.

At the time, NBE3U felt like an inconvenience. It was another mandatory course, another set of assignments, and another thing I had to get through before graduating. I didn't appreciate what I was learning because, to me, it seemed like too much reconciliation.

I couldn't have been more wrong.

In 2025, I moved to Indianapolis, USA to begin my undergraduate degree.

On September 30, I wore my bright orange tube top to class. I didn't think twice about it. It was Orange Shirt Day, the National Day for Truth and Reconciliation, and I had grown accustomed to participate.

I sat down in class and one of my friends burst out laughing.

"Evelyn you look like a pylon."

I stared back at him, genuinely confused.

"Dude...it's Orange Shirt Day?"

He looked at me like I had just made it up.

"What are you talking about?"

That was the moment I realized something that had somehow never occurred to me: Americans don't observe Orange Shirt Day the way Canadians do.

But that was only the beginning of the conversation.

As I began explaining the significance of the day, I realized my friends genuinely knew very little about the history of Indigenous peoples in North America. They had never heard of the residential school system. They didn't know about the children who were forcibly separated from their families, the attempts to erase their languages and cultures, or the generations of trauma that followed.

When I explained it to them, they were horrified. And I was horrified that they had never heard about it.

Of course, the United States has its own history of Indigenous boarding schools, forced assimilation, and violence against Native American communities. But that history wasn't something my friends could readily explain either.

Suddenly, I found myself in the unfamiliar position of being the person educating others about something I had once complained about having to learn. The things I had considered common knowledge were only common because someone had made the deliberate decision to teach them to me.

Education shapes what we consider normal, what we recognize as injustice, and whose experiences we believe deserve attention. When something is consistently included in a curriculum, students begin to understand that it is part of the history they are responsible for knowing. When it is absent, people can grow up without ever realizing that there is something they haven't been taught.

That doesn't necessarily mean they are indifferent or unwilling to learn. My friends weren't. They were curious, sympathetic, and genuinely disturbed by what I told them. They simply hadn't been given the same educational foundation I had.

The more time I spend in the United States, the more I realize that this isn't an isolated experience.

It's not just Indigenous history. It's drug and alcohol awareness, health education, and even history itself.

Growing up in Ontario, health education was something we were exposed to practically our entire lives. By the fourth grade, we were already learning about the effects of substance use. As we got older, those lessons became more detailed. I remember having to give presentations on different drugs, researching their effects on the brain and body, and learning about the physical and psychological consequences of addiction.

At the time, it was just another assignment.

I wasn't particularly excited to stand in front of my classmates and explain the dangers of substance abuse. I didn't think there was anything especially remarkable about being taught what alcohol does to your liver or how addiction can affect someone's life.

I assumed everyone learned this stuff.

So imagine my surprise when I started talking to American students and discovered that some of them had only taken a single semester or year of health education in high school.

One class.

And these are just a few examples of hundreds of conversations I've had since moving to the USA.

Of course, this doesn't mean that American students are inherently less knowledgeable, or that Canadian education is somehow perfect. I've met incredibly intelligent Americans who know far more about history and politics than I do. And I can think of plenty of things my own education failed to teach me.

What I've learned is that **Knowledge isn't something we're automatically born with. It's something we're given the opportunity to acquire.**

And those opportunities aren't distributed equally.

I think we tend to underestimate the influence that education has on how we see the world. We spend so much of our childhoods in classrooms that learning becomes a chore rather than a privilege. We complain about the homework, the assignments, the mandatory readings, and the subjects we don't particularly care about.

We rarely stop to consider what it would mean if those lessons had never been taught to us at all.

Looking back, I realize that NBE3U wasn't just a course about Indigenous literature. It was part of something much bigger: an effort to ensure that students graduated with an understanding of histories, perspectives, and experiences they might otherwise never encounter.

Was it perfectly executed? Absolutely not.

Do I still think some of the assignments were ridiculous? Yes.

And do I still stand by my opinion that the book I read for my final project was terrible? 100%

But I no longer think that disliking a course means it wasn't worth taking.

When I was 16, I saw NBE3U as something being forced into my education.

At 18, standing in an Indianapolis classroom wearing a bright orange shirt, I realized just how fortunate I was that it had been.

Because the most valuable things we learn in school aren't always the things we remember studying.

It's the things we forget we ever had to learn in the first place.
