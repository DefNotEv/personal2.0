title: How I ruined my life at 16
date: 2024-09

_On a scale of zero to six, how sensitive are you to noise?_

I was about to say zero—until I heard the buzz of the fan. And the ticking clock. And the clacking laptop keys.

"I think I'm a six."

After a never-ending list of questions, my doctor closed her laptop and faced me.

_So, this is not ideal._

Junior year was the worst year of my life. I had overloaded myself with Grade 12 courses and every extracurricular imaginable. One wrong move, and my chances of going to a good university would disappear.

Then came a Grade 3 concussion.

It wasn’t a visible injury that sidelined me from sports; it was an invisible one that halted my entire life. I was told not to think, not to stress, not to try.

Everything went on pause. I was told not to stress, as it would make my recovery longer. Recovery meant lying in the dark and doing nothing. But I couldn’t accept that, so I kept studying in secret—until my doctor caught on. My determination had only delayed my healing.

I ended up stuck at home for 21 days.

On the 14th day, I was cleared to take short walks outside. Desperate for fresh air, I ventured out into the peak of Canadian winter. I didn’t even make it two blocks before collapsing on a bench by a nearby public school. As I sat there, exhausted, the sound of children laughing behind me filled the cold air. For the first time in months, I felt peace.

I started reflecting on how overwhelmed I’d been my whole high school career. I’d choked myself with pressure to reach my goals, forgetting why I set them in the first place.

I joined clubs I didn’t care about, thinking they’d “look good.” I chased titles and competitions instead of passions. Somewhere along the way, I forgot why I started.

I loved robotics because I loved to innovate. I organized Apocalypse because I believed in making STEM accessible.

How could I forget? It wasn’t about winning competitions, organizing the biggest hackathon, or padding my resume—it was about doing things I cared about.

When I was finally cleared to return to activities after 39 days, I made a promise to myself: no more doing things just because they look impressive. I quit five clubs and turned down several high-paying internships. People thought I was crazy to walk away from such “amazing opportunities,” but for the first time, I had found something better.

I found myself.

Returning to school came with long-term effects: slower thinking, trouble concentrating, constant fatigue. But I’d gained something greater than speed or productivity: clarity. I learned to say no. To take things slower. To focus on what genuinely matters.

I stopped trying to be perfect. I ran for student council, regardless of if it fit the “ideal STEM student” image I’d created for myself. I joined varsity sports because I loved playing. I chose to focus on what I enjoy.

So, now that I've recovered, how sensitive am I to noise?

Unfortunately, still a six.

But now, I see it as a strength that reminds me to pause, filter out the distractions, and focus on what truly matters. The reality is: my concussion didn’t end my life. It helped me begin a new one where I’m not chasing perfection, but purpose.
